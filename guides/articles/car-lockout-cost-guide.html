What affects the cost of a car lockout?

A simple roadside unlock may be priced as a service call, while after-hours dispatch, long travel distance, difficult entry or key-related work can increase the total. Ask what the quoted service includes before the technician is sent.

Updated September 2026Roadside lockout guide

Service-call or dispatch charge

The base fee usually covers travel to the vehicle and a standard non-destructive entry attempt. The amount can vary by provider, distance and local market.

After-hours service

Nights, weekends and holidays may carry an additional charge. Ask whether the quote you receive already includes the timing surcharge.

Difficult entry and key systems

Some vehicles take more time or specialized tools to access without damage. Key replacement, transponder programming, broken-key extraction and lock repair are different services from a basic vehicle unlock and can be priced separately.

Check benefits before paying cash

Roadside assistance may be included through an auto insurer, vehicle manufacturer, auto club or another membership. Check whether the benefit covers lockout service and whether you must use a network provider or request reimbursement afterward.

Ask for a total dispatch price

Before agreeing to service, ask for the expected total, what could change it and whether there is a fee if the technician cannot open the vehicle.

Watch for locksmith bait-and-switch tactics

Emergency locksmith scams often exploit the fact that a locked-out driver wants the problem solved immediately. The Better Business Bureau has warned about a common pattern where an unusually low phone quote becomes a much higher price after the technician arrives.

  • Make the business identify itself. Ask for the company's specific name, not a generic "locksmith services" answer. Confirm the technician is from the company you called and ask for identification when appropriate.
  • Get the total or pricing formula before work starts. Ask about the service call, labor, mileage, after-hours charge and any key-related work. If the onsite price is dramatically different from the phone quote, you can decline before work begins when it is safe to do so.
  • Do not authorize destructive work casually. Drilling, lock destruction, key replacement or programming can turn a basic unlock into a much larger bill. Ask why a non-destructive entry method is not appropriate before authorizing destructive work.
  • Be suspicious of an extremely cheap teaser price. A quote that sounds far below every other provider may cover only the trip charge rather than the actual unlock. Ask what the quoted number includes.
  • Get an itemized invoice. The receipt should identify the business and separate labor, service-call, mileage, parts and other charges. Keep the text or screenshot of the original quote.
  • Avoid hard-to-reverse payment demands. Gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers or pressure to pay an unexplained personal account are serious red flags. The FTC advises consumers to be especially cautious when someone creates urgency and dictates a hard-to-recover payment method.
  • Use a known provider when possible. Roadside clubs, insurers and vehicle manufacturers may dispatch network providers. If you search on your own, verify the business rather than assuming the first sponsored search result is a local company.
If the technician pressures you: You do not have to accept a surprise price merely because someone has arrived. If no emergency exists and no work has begun, step away, call another provider or your roadside-assistance company, and compare.
Emergency: If a child, vulnerable person or pet is locked in dangerous temperatures or another life-safety emergency exists, contact emergency services immediately rather than delaying for price comparison.
