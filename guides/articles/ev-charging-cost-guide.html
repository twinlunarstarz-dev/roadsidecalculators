How to calculate EV charging cost

EV charging cost depends on how much energy the vehicle needs and what you pay for each kilowatt-hour. A useful estimate also accounts for the difference between energy stored in the battery and energy drawn from the grid.

Updated September 2026EV ownership guide

Start with the change in state of charge

If a 60 kWh usable battery moves from 20% to 80%, the battery gains 60% of its usable capacity, or about 36 kWh. That is the energy stored in the battery, not necessarily the total energy purchased from the charger.

Account for charging losses

Charging equipment and the vehicle's onboard systems are not perfectly efficient. Some energy becomes heat or is used by supporting systems, so energy drawn from the grid can be higher than the energy added to the battery. Temperature, power level and vehicle design can affect losses.

Multiply grid energy by the price per kWh

At home, use the electricity rate that actually applies to charging, including time-of-use rates when relevant. At public stations, use the posted price or membership price. Some stations also charge session, parking or idle fees.

Calculate driving cost per 100 miles

EV efficiency is commonly shown in kWh per 100 miles. If the vehicle uses 30 kWh/100 miles and electricity costs $0.20/kWh, the simple energy cost is about $6 per 100 miles. Weather, speed, HVAC use, terrain and towing can change real-world consumption.

Home versus public charging

Home charging often uses a residential electricity rate, while public fast charging may carry a higher energy price in exchange for much faster charging. The cheapest option depends on local rates, memberships and when or where you charge.

Avoid public-charging payment scams and surprise fees

Most charging sessions are routine, but public chargers combine physical equipment, mobile apps and payment screens—exactly the kind of environment where a tampered QR code or unclear pricing can cause problems.

  • Inspect QR codes before scanning. The FTC warns that scammers can place a fraudulent QR-code sticker over a legitimate code in a public place. If the code looks covered, crooked, newly stickered or otherwise tampered with, do not use it.
  • Prefer the charging network's official app or a known website. If you already know the network, open its official app yourself or type the known web address instead of trusting an unfamiliar QR destination.
  • Check the URL before entering payment information. Look for misspellings, strange domains or a page that does not match the charging network. Do not download an app from an unexpected QR-code prompt; use your phone's official app store.
  • Read the complete price before starting. Confirm whether billing is per kWh, per minute or session, and look for idle fees, parking charges, taxes, membership pricing or time-of-day differences. A cheap energy rate can still become expensive if other charges apply.
  • Do the membership math. A paid charging plan can save money for frequent users, but compare the monthly fee with the actual per-kWh discount before subscribing because a membership is not automatically cheaper.
  • Use official support contact information. If a charger fails or billing looks wrong, use the phone number or support channel shown in the official network app or on untampered equipment. Be cautious of stickers that appear to cover an original support number.
  • Save the session record. Keep the station ID, start/end time, energy delivered and receipt if the price is disputed. Screenshots are useful when an app later shows a different charge.
If a QR page asks for unusual information or urgent payment: close it and verify through the network's official app or website. The FTC advises consumers to inspect QR destinations carefully and avoid entering financial information on suspicious or spoofed pages.
